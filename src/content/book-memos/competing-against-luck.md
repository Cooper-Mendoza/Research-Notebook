Most business books have one good idea. This one has a good idea and a harder second idea that most readers miss.

The first idea is Jobs-to-be-Done: customers don't buy products, they hire them to make progress in their lives. You've probably heard this. The milkshake story is famous. The insight is real and useful.

The second idea is the one that costs something to believe: correlation between product attributes and purchase decisions is not just unreliable — it's actively misleading. The features customers ask for in surveys are rarely the features that cause them to hire or fire a product.

If you fully believe the second idea, it changes what you do with customer research. You stop asking "which features would you like?" and start asking "walk me through the last time you switched." You stop designing for attributes and start designing for progress.

Most product teams I've seen apply JTBD pick up the vocabulary and leave the epistemology behind. They add "jobs" to their research process and otherwise continue to treat feature preferences as informative. That's not the framework working — it's the framework domesticated.

## Where the book falls short

The methodology section is weaker than the theory section.

Christensen et al. are very good at explaining why traditional segmentation fails. They are less clear about how to actually identify jobs in practice. The advice is somewhere between "conduct interviews" and "develop a feel for it." That's honest about the difficulty, but it leaves practitioners without much traction.

The hard part of JTBD is not the concept — it's the craft of a research interview that surfaces causality rather than preference. That craft is not in this book.

## What I took away

The most practical thing I took from this book is a question to ask before starting any customer research: "Are we trying to explain past behavior or predict future preferences?"

Explaining past behavior is tractable. Predicting future preferences is very hard. Jobs-to-be-Done works because it keeps you in the first category. The moment you drift toward feature preference surveys, you've left the framework.

I also came away with higher skepticism about most user research I've seen cited in business cases. If the research asked what customers *want*, it probably doesn't tell you what they'll actually *do*. The question matters more than the response.
